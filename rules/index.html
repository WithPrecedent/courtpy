    Instructions: the rules that parse court opinions¶

    courtpy finds information in court opinions with rules written in CSV files, which can be opened and changed in Excel, Google Sheets, or any spreadsheet. Each row is one rule. You can change these files, or make your own and pass them to courtpy (rulebooks = my_rules.csv in a settings file, or courtpy.parse(folder, rulebooks = ["federal", "my_rules.csv"]) in Python).

    The rules are in three folders:

    • general has the rules that fit opinions of any jurisdiction.
    • federal has the rules that fit only federal opinions. A file here adds to the general file of the same name and does not repeat its rules. A topic with no federal rules (such as citations) has no file here.
    • sources has a folder for each source of cases (courtlistener and lexis_nexis) with the rules that fit only its cases: header.csv, and for Lexis-Nexis, the rules that divide a case into its parts, with a file for each kind of court (federal_appellate.csv, for the federal courts of appeals).

    Some files of federal are tables that rules are made from, or that courtpy reads, rather than rules:

    • courts.csv has a row for each federal court: courtpy's number for it, its name, its name as a label for tables and plots (feature_name), its level (high, appellate, trial, or specialty), its circuit, CourtListener's id for it (courtlistener_name), and patterns of the names that the Federal Judicial Center and opinions use for it (see courtpy.courts). The header rules find each case's court with it (by CourtListener's id, or by the names in a Lexis-Nexis header) and the court whose decision is reviewed (by the names in the history), so a court added to it is found without changing the rules, and add_circuit finds each case's circuit with it.
    • agencies.csv is a topic of rules of its own (below), and the header rules are made from it too, to find the agencies named in each party and the history. In a party, an agency's name must end the party or come before a comma, semicolon, closing parenthesis, or "et al.", so that a company named for an agency (such as "FMC WYOMING CORPORATION") is not taken for it.
    • presidents.csv has the term of each president, with the president's party (-1 for Democratic and 1 for Republican). add_executive and courtpy.politics.presidents read it, so a new president can be added to it.
    Topic Files What it does
    lexis_nexis sources/lexis_nexis/federal_appellate.csv Divides a Lexis-Nexis case into its header and opinions, and finds each part of the header (the parties, court, docket number, dates, history, counsel, disposition, citation, judges, and author). CourtListener cases do not need it, because CourtListener stores those parts separately.
    courtlistener_header, lexis_nexis_header general/header.csv, federal/agencies.csv, sources/courtlistener/header.csv or sources/lexis_nexis/header.csv, federal/courts.csv Finds variables in the parts of the header: each party's role, the agencies named in each party and the history (party1_agencies, party2_agencies, and history_agencies), the disposition, the counsel, citations, docket numbers, and the names of the judges (general/header.csv). Every source's rules find the trial or specialized court whose decision is reviewed in the history (lower_court_num and lower_court_name, from the courts' names in courts.csv). The Lexis-Nexis rules also find the court's number and name (court_num and court_name, from the court's names in courts.csv), the subsequent history, the Lexis citation, and the notice of an unpublished opinion; CourtListener cases have their court's number and name, and the court below's, from CourtListener's ids for them. When a CourtListener case has no roles in its header (as recent ones often do not), its rules read them from the caption at the start of the opinion. Each source's header rules are applied to its cases (header, with only the general rules and agencies, to a source with none of its own). Always applied.
    general general/general.csv Finds the decision that the opinion states ("AFFIRMED." or "we reverse"), its words about dispositions anywhere, procedure (such as pro se, mandamus, and mootness), and standards of review. Always applied.
    citations general/citations.csv Finds citations to cases, statutes, regulations, the Constitution, legislative materials, and other sources (citations_...).
    civil general/civil.csv, federal/civil.csv Finds the civil issues discussed: general civil issues (civil_...), torts (tort_...), contracts (contract_...), intellectual property (ip_...), and writs (writ_...). Each prefix is a case type (see code_case_type), so a new prefix (such as immigration_) is a case type too.
    constitutional general/constitutional.csv Finds the constitutional issues discussed (constitutional_...).
    criminal general/criminal.csv, federal/criminal.csv Finds the criminal issues discussed (criminal_...).
    review federal/review.csv Finds what kind of review an appeal is: from a federal district court (review_district), of an agency (review_agency), or of the Board of Immigration Appeals or an immigration judge (review_immigration). For an appeal from a district court, it finds the district judge as the opening names them (district_text) and their name (district_judges).
    agencies federal/agencies.csv Finds every federal agency that the caption, the history, or the opinion names (agencies), by its name or a common abbreviation, as the agencies' names separated by "; " (such as "Internal Revenue Service; United States Tax Court"). The agencies are those of the Federal Judicial Center's Integrated Database (as fjc-idb lists them), and each rule's note gives fjc-idb's code.

    Each topic's rules are its general file and then its federal file, in that order. The source's header rules and general are applied to every case, whether or not they are named. The others can be chosen by name: rulebooks = civil, criminal in a settings file (or courtpy.parse(folder, rulebooks = ["civil", "criminal"]) in Python) parses with the header rules, general, civil, and criminal. The "federal" rulebook (the default) is every topic from general on, in the order above. A file named more than once is applied once.

    The columns¶

    The files use the layout of amos's parse_all rulebooks, which courtpy uses to apply them. (Files in courtpy's older layout, with target, variable, ignorecase, and dotall columns and the kinds flag, count, label, labels, section, excerpts, and remove, still work: amos reads them as the rules they stand for.)

    Column Meaning
    column The section of text to search, such as opinion, party1, or disposition. List several, separated by commas, to search each of them.
    name The name of the column that the rule makes. Rules with the same name are combined. A split rule names two or more.
    kind What the rule does (see below).
    pattern A regular expression to search for. Plain words work too.
    value For a category or categories rule, the value to record. Otherwise leave it blank.
    flags Any of ignorecase (to ignore the difference between capital and lower-case letters), dotall (for . in the pattern to also match line breaks), multiline, and verbose, separated by commas. Optional.
    separator What goes between several values in one cell, for categories, matches, and names rules. Optional: a comma and a space if blank. Every courtpy rule that makes several values uses "; ", since names and agencies can have commas in them.
    note Anything you want to say about the rule. courtpy ignores it.

    The kinds of rules¶

    Kind Makes Example
    flags TRUE if the pattern appears, and otherwise FALSE. opinion,criminal_firearm,flags,[Pp]ossession of (a )?[Ff]irearm
    counts The number of times the pattern appears. opinion,habeas_mentions,counts,habeas
    matches Every match, separated by the separator. opinion,citations_case,matches,\d+ (?:F\.(?:[23]d\|4th)\|U\.S\.) \d+,,,;
    match The first match. A later rule can search it as a new section of text. header,counsel,match,\nCOUNSEL\:.*?(?=\n\n)
    category The rule's value if the pattern appears. The first rule for a name that matches is used. court,court_num,category,COURT OF APPEALS FOR THE FIRST CIRCUIT,1 (as made from courts.csv)
    categories The value of every rule for the name whose pattern appears, once each, in the order of the rules and separated by the separator (such as "Internal Revenue Service; United States Tax Court"). A value cannot have the separator. opinion,agencies,categories,NLRB\b(?<![\w-]NLRB),National Labor Relations Board
    names The names in the text, found by splitting it wherever the pattern matches, and separated by the separator. The pattern should match what comes between names: titles, filler words, and punctuation. judges,panel_judges,names,\b(?:BEFORE\|CIRCUIT\|JUDGES?\|AND)\b\|[,;:],,,;
    split New sections, named in name: the text before and after the first match. party,"party1, party2",split,\Wv\.\W
    replace The text with every match replaced by the value (a blank value removes the matches). Without a name, it changes the section for the rules below it. party1,,replace,\bET AL\b

    amos has more kinds (such as fields, which makes a column for each labeled part of a text); see its amos.parsers module.

    Things to know¶

    • Rules run from top to bottom. A rule can search a section made by a rule above it (for example, party1 and party2 exist only after the split rule that makes them). Each rule searches every case at once.
    • Text is put on one line first. Before the federal rules run, every section except those whose names end in _lines has its line breaks and extra spaces replaced with single spaces, so that a phrase broken across two lines is still found. (The lexis_nexis rules run before this, so they can use line breaks, written \n.) The header rules run next, and then the federal rules.
    • Names are cleaned. A names rule makes the text upper-case and removes periods and apostrophes before splitting it, so write its pattern in capital letters.
    • Several values are text. The names, matches, and categories of a case are one cell of text, with "; " between them, such as "LYNCH; HOWARD; THOMPSON". The split_values technique turns them into lists or into a column for each value, and amos's lists_to_rows into a row for each value.
    • Rules that start with letters are fast. Python finds a pattern that begins with letters by searching for them, which is many times faster than trying the pattern at every character. A pattern that begins with \b, a group, or a class, or that ignores case (the ignorecase flag), loses that. So agencies.csv matches with case, writing a name in its capitalized form and in capitals (Federal\s+Trade\s+Commission|FEDERAL\s+TRADE\s+COMMISSION), and puts the check that an abbreviation is a whole word after its letters (NLRB\b(?<![\w-]NLRB) rather than \bNLRB\b). The check also skips an abbreviation right after a hyphen, which is usually a judge's initials at the end of a docket number (such as "06-CR-126-CAB").
    • Curly quotes and dashes are UTF-8. Some patterns have characters such as ’ (in ['’], an apostrophe of either kind) or – and —. If they show as â€™ or 'â], the spreadsheet opened the file in another encoding: open it again as UTF-8 (in LibreOffice, choose "Unicode (UTF-8)" as the character set when it asks).
    • Missing sections are not errors. If a case has no section that a rule searches (such as future in a CourtListener case), a flag is FALSE, a count is 0, and the other kinds are blank.
    • A match rule does not replace a section made by a rule above it. So two match rules for one name are a first choice and a fallback: the header rules make panel_text from the opinion's "Before ..." line, and only otherwise from the header's judges.
    • Three sets of variables record the disposition. disposition_... come from the header's disposition, decision_... from the decision that the opinion states ("AFFIRMED.", "VACATED AND REMANDED.", or "we reverse"), and opinion_... from those words anywhere in the opinion, which may be about an earlier decision or a standard of review ("we will reverse only if"). The code_outcome coder uses the first of the three that a case has.
    • Check your rules with courtpy rules my_rules.csv in a terminal. It reports any row that is not a valid rule, by its row number, and lists the columns the rules make.
    • Save files as CSV in UTF-8 ("CSV UTF-8" in Excel), so that symbols such as § are kept.

    Sections of text¶

    These sections can be targets in the federal rules (and your own):

    Section Lexis-Nexis CourtListener
    party The caption. The full case name.
    party1, party2 Made by the split rule in general/header.csv. The same.
    court The court's line in the header, which courts.csv numbers. The court's full name. (court_num comes from CourtListener's id for the court.)
    docket_number The docket line. The docket number (if dockets were downloaded).
    citation The citation line. The case's citations.
    dates Every line with a date, with "; " between them. (Dates come from CourtListener's data.)
    history "PRIOR HISTORY". The procedural history and the court appealed from.
    future "SUBSEQUENT HISTORY". (None.)
    notice "NOTICE". (None: publication comes from CourtListener's precedential status.)
    counsel "COUNSEL". The attorneys.
    disposition "DISPOSITION". The disposition (often blank).
    judges "JUDGES". The judges.
    opinion_by "OPINION BY". The authors of the majority (or lead) opinions.
    concurring_lines, dissenting_lines Lines about concurrences and dissents, with "; " between them. The authors of concurrences and dissents.
    posture, syllabus (None.) CourtListener's posture and syllabus.
    opinion Everything after the line "OPINION". The text of every opinion, the majority first.

    The header rules make these sections from the opinion, which your own rules can search too:

    Section Holds
    opening_lines The first 4,000 characters of the opinion (not kept in the table).
    caption CourtListener only: the header's caption, if it names an appellant or appellee. Otherwise, the caption in the opinion's opening, from the first party's role through the second's (such as "Plaintiff-Appellee, v. JOHN DOE, Defendant-Appellant").
    caption1, caption2 CourtListener only: the caption before and after "v.", in the order of party1 and party2.
    panel_text The panel as the opinion's opening names it ("Before SMITH, JONES, and BROWN, Circuit Judges"). Otherwise, the judges section.
    decision The decision as the opinion states it.

    Rules of your own¶

    A file of your own can be named by its path wherever a rulebook can be named. To name it like a built-in rulebook instead (rulebooks = federal, review), make each file in a folder a rulebook with courtpy.rules.register_rulebooks(folder) when your study's package is imported. Each file is a rulebook named for its stem, and each rulebook is also a technique (such as parse_review). With several folders, each topic applies the file of the same name in each folder, in order, as courtpy applies its general and then its federal files. See Building a Study.
